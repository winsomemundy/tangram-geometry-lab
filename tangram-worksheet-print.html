Tangram Geometry Lab

Seven flat pieces are cut from one 10 cm square. You will measure them, compare them, and prove that no area is lost when the square is taken apart.

What you will do

  • Part A: find the perimeter and area of each piece.
  • Part B: identify congruent and similar figures.
  • Part C: find the side, perimeter and area of the whole square.
  • Part D: show that the seven areas add up to the area of the square.
  • Part E: investigate more patterns.

You need: a pencil, a ruler, a calculator and scissors. Round answers to 2 decimal places.

A short history of the tangram

The tangram is a dissection puzzle: a square cut into seven pieces called tans. The rules are simple. Use all seven pieces, lay them flat, and do not let them overlap. People have built thousands of silhouettes this way: animals, people, boats, letters and numbers.

Song dynasty
Historians often trace the idea to yanjitu, sets of banquet tables described by the scholar Huang Bosi that could be pushed together into different shapes. A later Ming-dynasty version used triangular "butterfly" tables.
c. 1800
The puzzle appears in China as qīqiǎobǎn, "seven boards of skill." The oldest surviving tangram books were printed around 1813.
1815–1820
Trading ships carried tangram sets to America and Europe. A "tangram craze" swept Europe around 1817–1818, with puzzle books printed in many languages.
Mid-1800s
The English word tangram appears. Nobody is sure where it comes from.
1903
Puzzle maker Sam Loyd published The Eighth Book of Tan, claiming the puzzle was 4,000 years old and invented by a god named Tan. Loyd made the whole story up, but the myth spread for decades.
1942
Mathematicians Fu Traing Wang and Chuan-Chih Hsiung proved that exactly 13 convex shapes (shapes with no dents) can be made from the seven tans.

Every tangram piece is built from the same right isosceles triangle. That makes the tangram a perfect tool for studying area, perimeter, angles and similarity.

Part E · More things to notice

Tick and complete at least two of these. Use your cut-out tangram from the last student page.

Area is kept, perimeter is not

Add up the perimeters of all seven pieces. Compare the total with the square's perimeter. Why is the total so much bigger?

The small triangle as a unit

How many small triangles would cover each piece? How many would cover the whole square?

Same area, different shapes

Build a rectangle, a triangle and a parallelogram using all seven pieces. Each has an area of 100 cm². Do they have the same perimeter?

Perimeter twins

Two different pieces have the same area and the same perimeter. Which ones? Are they congruent?

Angles in 45° steps

List every angle in all seven pieces. What do you notice? Why does this help the pieces fit together?

The piece you must flip

Which piece cannot match its own mirror image just by sliding or turning? Try it with your cut-out pieces.

Where √2 hides

Several lengths are multiples of √2 ≈ 1.414. Could every side of every piece be a whole number of centimetres? Why or why not?

Part B · Congruent and similar figures

  1. A and B are congruent (legs 7.07 cm, hypotenuse 10 cm). D and E are congruent (legs 3.54 cm, hypotenuse 5 cm). These are the only congruent pairs.
  2. All five triangles (A, B, C, D, E) are similar: each is a right isosceles triangle with angles 45°, 45°, 90°. F and G are not similar to any other piece.
  3. Small → medium: 7.07 ÷ 5 ≈ 1.41 (√2). Medium → large: 10 ÷ 7.07 ≈ 1.41 (√2). Small → large: 10 ÷ 5 = 2.
  4. Areas are 6.25, 12.5 and 25 cm², a ratio of 1 : 2 : 4. The area is multiplied by k². With k = √2 the area doubles; with k = 2 it is multiplied by 4.
  5. No. They have equal areas but different shapes and angles, so they are neither congruent nor similar. Equal area does not mean congruent.

Part C · The completed square

The top side is the hypotenuse of A: 10 cm. The right side is D's hypotenuse (5 cm) plus one leg of C (5 cm) = 10 cm. Perimeter = 4 × 10 = 40 cm. Area = 10 × 10 = 100 cm².

Part D · Do the pieces add up?

25 + 25 + 12.5 + 6.25 + 6.25 + 12.5 + 12.5 = 100 cm² = area of the square. Fractions: A and B are ¼ each; C, F and G are ⅛ each; D and E are 1/16 each. ¼ + ¼ + ⅛ + ⅛ + ⅛ + 1/16 + 1/16 = 16/16 = 1. The pieces cover the square exactly once with no gaps or overlaps, so their areas must add to the square's area.

Part E · More things to notice

  • Area is kept, perimeter is not: total ≈ 2(24.14) + 17.07 + 2(12.07) + 14.14 + 17.07 = 120.71 cm, about three times 40 cm. Inside edges belong to two pieces and get counted twice, but they are not on the outside of the square.
  • The small triangle as a unit: large = 4, medium = 2, square = 2, parallelogram = 2, small = 1. The whole square is 4 + 4 + 2 + 1 + 1 + 2 + 2 = 16 small triangles, which matches 100 ÷ 6.25 = 16.
  • Same area, different shapes: no. Shapes with the same area can have different perimeters. Of all rectangles with a given area, the square has the smallest perimeter.
  • Perimeter twins: the medium triangle C and the parallelogram G (12.5 cm² and 17.07 cm each). They are not congruent.
  • Angles in 45° steps: every angle is 45°, 90° or 135°. Angles meeting at a point must total 360° (or 180° along a straight edge), and multiples of 45° combine easily to make those totals.
  • The piece you must flip: the parallelogram G. It has no line of symmetry, so its mirror image can only be matched by flipping it over. Every other piece has a line of symmetry.
  • Where √2 hides: no. In a right isosceles triangle the hypotenuse is always √2 times a leg, and √2 is irrational, so the legs and the hypotenuse can never both be whole numbers.
  • The 13 convex shapes: 1 triangle, 6 quadrilaterals, 2 pentagons and 4 hexagons (Wang and Hsiung, 1942).
