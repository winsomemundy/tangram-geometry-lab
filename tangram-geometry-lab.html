Tangram Geometry Lab

Seven pieces, one square.

Seven flat pieces cut from one 10 cm square. You will measure them, compare them, and prove that nothing is lost when you take the square apart.

You need: a calculator, a pencil, and a paper tangram if you have one Round to: 2 decimal places

Teachers: the answer key is hidden until you show it. Students can check table answers as they go.

Background

A short history of the tangram

The tangram is a dissection puzzle: a square cut into seven pieces called tans. The rules are simple. Use all seven pieces, lay them flat, and do not let them overlap. With those rules people have built thousands of silhouettes: animals, people, boats, letters and numbers.

  1. Song dynastyHistorians often trace the idea to yanjitu, sets of banquet tables described by the Song scholar Huang Bosi that could be pushed together into different shapes. A Ming-dynasty version used triangular "butterfly" tables. These were furniture, not puzzles, but they used the same idea of rearranging fixed parts.
  2. c. 1800The puzzle itself appears in China, called qīqiǎobǎn (七巧板), "seven boards of skill." The oldest surviving tangram books were printed in the early 1800s, around 1813.
  3. 1815–1820Trading ships carried tangram sets and books to America and Europe. A "tangram craze" swept Europe around 1817–1818, with puzzle books printed in English, French, German, Italian and Danish. Popular stories claim famous people of the time played it; many of these stories are hard to verify.
  4. Mid-1800sThe English word tangram appears. Nobody is sure where it comes from. One guess joins "Tang" (an old name for China) with "-gram" (something drawn or written).
  5. 1903American puzzle maker Sam Loyd published The Eighth Book of Tan, claiming the puzzle was 4,000 years old and invented by a god named Tan. It was a joke. Loyd made the whole history up, but the myth spread for decades.
  6. 1942Mathematicians Fu Traing Wang and Chuan-Chih Hsiung proved that exactly 13 convex shapes (shapes with no dents) can be made from the seven tans.

Today tangrams are used in classrooms around the world because every piece is built from the same right isosceles triangle. That makes them a perfect tool for studying area, perimeter, angles and similarity.

Part B

Congruent and similar figures

Congruent figures have exactly the same shape and size: one fits perfectly on top of the other. Similar figures have the same shape (equal angles, sides in the same ratio) but can be different sizes. Every pair of congruent figures is also similar.

  1. Which pairs of pieces are congruent? Explain how you know.

    Write your answer here, or on paper.

    A and B are congruent (legs 7.07, hypotenuse 10). D and E are congruent (legs 3.54, hypotenuse 5). These are the only congruent pairs.

  2. Which pieces are similar to each other? List every group.

    All five triangles (A, B, C, D, E) are similar. Each is a right isosceles triangle with angles 45°, 45°, 90°. The square F and the parallelogram G are not similar to any other piece.

  3. Find the scale factor from a small triangle to the medium triangle, and from the medium triangle to a large triangle. (Divide matching sides, for example hypotenuse ÷ hypotenuse.)

    Small → medium: 7.07 ÷ 5 ≈ 1.41 (exactly √2). Medium → large: 10 ÷ 7.07 ≈ 1.41 (√2). Small → large: 10 ÷ 5 = 2.

  4. Compare the areas of the small, medium and large triangles. When the side lengths are multiplied by the scale factor k, what happens to the area?

    Areas are 6.25, 12.5 and 25 cm², a ratio of 1 : 2 : 4. Each time the sides grow by √2, the area doubles, because the area is multiplied by k² = (√2)² = 2. From small to large, k = 2, so the area is multiplied by 2² = 4.

  5. The medium triangle C, the square F and the parallelogram G all have the same area. Are any of them congruent? Are any of them similar? Explain.

    No. They have equal area but different shapes and different angles (a triangle, a square and a parallelogram), so they are neither congruent nor similar. Equal area does not mean congruent.

Part E · Go further

More things to notice

Pick at least two of these. Use a paper tangram if you can.

Area is kept, perimeter is not

Add up the perimeters of all seven pieces. Compare the total with the square's perimeter of 40 cm. Why is the total so much bigger?

Total ≈ 2(24.14) + 17.07 + 2(12.07) + 14.14 + 17.07 = 120.71 cm, about 3 times 40 cm. Inside edges are shared by two pieces and get counted twice, but they are not on the outside of the square.

The small triangle as a unit

How many small triangles would cover each piece? How many would cover the whole square?

Large = 4, medium = 2, square = 2, parallelogram = 2, small = 1. The whole square is 4 + 4 + 2 + 1 + 1 + 2 + 2 = 16 small triangles, matching 100 ÷ 6.25 = 16.

Same area, different shapes

Build a rectangle, a triangle and a parallelogram using all seven pieces. Each has an area of 100 cm². Do they have the same perimeter?

No. Shapes with the same area can have very different perimeters. A square has the smallest perimeter of any rectangle with a given area.

Same perimeter twins

Two different pieces have the same area and the same perimeter. Which ones?

The medium triangle C and the parallelogram G: both 12.5 cm² and 17.07 cm. They are still not congruent.

Angles in 45° steps

List every angle in all seven pieces. What do you notice? Why does this help the pieces fit together?

Every angle is 45°, 90° or 135°, all multiples of 45°. Angles that meet at a point must add to 360° (or 180° along a straight edge), and multiples of 45° combine easily to make those totals.

The piece you must flip

The parallelogram is the only piece that cannot match its own mirror image by sliding or turning. Try it. Some tangram pictures need it flipped over.

A parallelogram that is not a rectangle or rhombus has no line of symmetry, so its reflection can only be matched by flipping it over. Every other tan has a line of symmetry.

Where √2 hides

Several lengths are multiples of √2, which is irrational. Can a tangram cut from a 10 cm square have every side length be a whole number of centimetres?

No. In a right isosceles triangle the hypotenuse is always √2 times a leg, so the legs and the hypotenuse can never both be whole numbers. That is why the decimals never quite "come out even."

The 13 convex shapes

Wang and Hsiung proved only 13 convex shapes can be made from all seven pieces. How many can you find? (Hint: the square, a rectangle, a triangle and a trapezoid are among them.)

The 13 are: 1 triangle, 6 quadrilaterals, 2 pentagons and 4 hexagons.
